## How to use this calculator

The results update as you type. Every field starts with an example value; change the ones you know.

- **The home:** the price, your deposit as a percentage, the mortgage rate and the term.
- **Buying and owning costs:** whether you'd be a first-time buyer (for Stamp Duty), other buying costs, buildings insurance, maintenance (a share of the home's value each year) and any service charge or ground rent.
- **Renting:** the monthly rent for a similar home, and how much you expect it to rise each year.
- **The future:** how long you'd stay, how fast you expect house prices to grow, what you'd earn on savings and investments, and the cost of selling.

The results show which choice leaves you better off after the years you'd stay, and by how much. They also show the year buying pulls ahead, your monthly mortgage payment and the cash you'd need up front, including Stamp Duty. A table compares your net worth, the money paid out, the home value and the mortgage left. Below the calculator, the chart shows your net worth for 30 years either way, and the table gives every year's figures.

## Example: a £300,000 home or £1,300 a month in rent

Say you're a first-time buyer choosing between a £300,000 home and renting a similar one for £1,300 a month. You'd put down a 15% deposit (£45,000) on a 25-year mortgage at 4.5%, which costs £1,417.37 a month. First-time buyer relief means no Stamp Duty, and other buying costs come to £2,500, so you'd need £47,500 up front.

In the first month, owning costs £1,696.54: the mortgage, plus £350 a year of buildings insurance and 1% a year for maintenance. That's £396.54 more than the rent, so the renter invests that difference along with the £47,500, earning 4% a year. House prices and rent both rise 3% a year, and selling costs 2%.

The result is sensitive to your assumptions. Over 10 years:

- **Moving home instead of buying for the first time** adds £5,000 of Stamp Duty and cuts buying's lead to £94,238.36.
- **Rent of £1,100** cuts buying's lead to £68,325.29.
- **A 6% investment return** cuts it to £81,944.41.
- **House prices growing 2% a year** cut it to £66,857.80.

## How it's calculated

The calculator follows both choices month by month for 30 years.

**If you buy**, you pay the deposit, Stamp Duty and other buying costs up front. Stamp Duty uses the current rates for England and Northern Ireland, with first-time buyer relief if you choose it. Each month you pay the mortgage (monthly rate = rate ÷ 12, standard repayment), maintenance as a share of the home's value, insurance and any service charge. Your net worth is:

**home value − selling costs − mortgage left + any money you invested**

**If you rent**, you invest the money a buyer would have paid up front. In any month when owning costs more than rent, you invest the difference. When rent costs more, the buyer invests the difference instead. Your net worth is your investments.

House prices grow each month at the equivalent of the yearly rate, and investments grow the same way at your chosen return. Rent rises once a year. Insurance and service charges rise with house prices. Council Tax is left out because you pay it whether you rent or buy. **Buying pulls ahead** in the first year from which buying stays ahead through year 30.

The calculator leaves out tax on investment gains and assumes the rates you enter stay the same, including the mortgage rate, even though most UK mortgage deals are fixed for only a few years. Figures are calculated at full precision and rounded for display.

## Tips for deciding

1. **Be honest about how long you'll stay.** Short stays rarely recover Stamp Duty and the costs of buying and selling.
2. **Compare like with like.** Use the rent for a home of the same size, area and quality as the one you'd buy.
3. **Use your real costs.** A conveyancing quote, a buildings insurance quote and your mortgage illustration will make the result more accurate than the example figures.
4. **Plan for rate changes.** When a fixed deal ends, your mortgage rate could rise. Try a higher rate to see how much it changes the answer.
5. **Count the renter's investing.** Renting comes out ahead only if you actually invest the money you'd have put into the home, ideally in an ISA.
6. **Think beyond the numbers.** Stability, freedom to decorate, flexibility to move and the work of maintaining a home all matter too.
