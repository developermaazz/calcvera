  - q: What is the super guarantee rate?
    a: Employers must pay at least 12% of your earnings into super from 1 July 2025. From 1 July 2026, it's paid with each pay day. There's a maximum contribution base of $270,830 a year in 2026–27.
  - q: How much can I contribute to super each year?
    a: In 2026–27, the concessional (before-tax) cap is $32,500, which includes your employer's contributions and any salary sacrifice. The non-concessional (after-tax) cap is $130,000. Contributions above the caps may be taxed extra.
  - q: Is salary sacrifice worth it?
    a: Salary sacrifice is taxed at 15% in your fund instead of your marginal tax rate, so for many people more of each dollar goes into super. In our example, sacrificing $5,000 a year adds about $703,030 by 67, or about $281,962 in today's dollars. You can't usually access it until retirement.
  - q: When can I access my super?
    a: Generally once you reach your preservation age and retire, or meet another condition of release. Your preservation age is 60 if you were born from 1 July 1964.
  - q: How much do fees affect my super?
    a: Fees come out every year, so they add up. In our example, paying $600 a year instead of $300 leaves about $49,626 less at retirement. Percentage-based investment fees matter too; use your fund's return after fees.

## How to use this calculator

The results update as you type.

- **You:** your age, the age you plan to retire, your yearly salary (not including super) and how much you expect it to grow each year.
- **Your super:** your balance today, any salary sacrifice or after-tax contributions, your fund's yearly return after investment fees and tax, its admin fees, and the inflation rate used for today's dollars.

The results show your projected balance at retirement, its value in today's dollars, and the totals paid in, earned and paid in fees. Below the calculator, the chart shows your balance at each age and the table gives each year's figures.

## Example: age 30, earning $85,000

Say you're 30 with $50,000 in super, earning $85,000 with 3% pay rises each year. Your fund returns 7% a year after investment fees and tax, and charges $300 a year in admin fees. You plan to retire at 67.

- **This year:** your employer pays 12%, or $10,200. After 15% contributions tax, $8,670 goes into your super.
- **At 67:** your super could reach **$2,627,436.23**, or **$1,053,778.09 in today's dollars**.
- **Where it comes from:** $573,730.51 of contributions after tax and $2,014,805.72 of investment earnings, less $11,100 of fees.

Small changes make a big difference over 37 years:

| Change | Balance at 67 | In today's dollars |
| --- | --- | --- |
| None (the example) | $2,627,436.23 | $1,053,778.09 |
| Salary sacrifice $5,000 a year | $3,330,465.81 | $1,335,740.09 |
| Return 6% instead of 7% | $2,070,121.42 | $830,257.49 |
| Fees $600 instead of $300 | $2,577,810.62 | $1,033,874.89 |

Starting later means less time for growth. At 40 with $150,000, the same salary and return would give $1,801,843.76 at 67 ($925,066.09 in today's dollars).

## How it's calculated

**Contributions each year:**

- Super guarantee = 12% of your salary, on earnings up to $270,830.
- Concessional contributions (super guarantee + salary sacrifice) are taxed 15% in the fund.
- After-tax contributions go in without further tax.

**Growth:** contributions and fees are spread across the year monthly. Each month, the balance earns the monthly equivalent of the yearly return:

**monthly rate = (1 + yearly return)^(1/12) − 1**

**Today's dollars** = balance at retirement ÷ (1 + inflation)^years.

Your salary grows each year, but the caps, the maximum contribution base and fees stay at today's values. The calculator doesn't include Division 293 tax for high incomes, insurance premiums inside super or government co-contributions. Returns vary from year to year and can be negative. Figures are calculated at full precision and rounded for display.

## Tips for your super

1. **Check your employer pays the right amount.** Your super guarantee should be 12% of your earnings; check your fund statements or myGov.
2. **Combine lost or duplicate accounts.** Paying fees on several funds eats into your balance.
3. **Compare fees and long-term returns.** A small difference each year compounds over decades.
4. **Consider salary sacrifice.** It's taxed at 15% instead of your marginal rate, but you can't spend it until retirement.
5. **Stay under the caps.** Your employer's contributions count towards the $32,500 concessional cap.
6. **Review your investment option.** Growth options usually aim for higher long-term returns but fall further in bad years.
